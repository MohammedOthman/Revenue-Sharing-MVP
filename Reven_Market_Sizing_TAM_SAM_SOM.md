# Reven — Market Sizing: TAM, SAM, SOM, and the Value Ceiling

**Audience:** Investors, including generalists. No prior knowledge of partnership software assumed.
**Purpose:** Show how big the market can get (the **market ceiling**), how much one customer can eventually be worth (the **value ceiling**), and what we can realistically capture — with every number's reasoning shown in plain language.
**Method source:** `Reven_Market_Architecture_Adoption_and_Economic_Potential_Report.docx` §12 (assumption ladder, account-based core model, product-stage layers, anti-inflation rules). This document applies that method to Reven's **actual pricing**: **SAR 50 per seat per month.**
**As-of:** 2026-08-17. Currency convention: **SAR 3.75 = USD 1** (the riyal's longstanding official peg). This is a reporting convention, not a forecast.

---

## 0. Read this first: the three numbers, in plain English

Investors see "TAM" used to mean almost anything. Here is exactly what we mean, and what we refuse to do.

| Term | Plain meaning | Our version |
|---|---|---|
| **TAM** — Total Addressable Market | If every company that genuinely has this problem bought the product, what would they pay us in a year? | The **ceiling**, not a plan |
| **SAM** — Serviceable Addressable Market | Of those, how many can we actually reach and serve *today* — right country, right product, right compliance, right sales route? | The **realistic field of play** |
| **SOM** — Serviceable Obtainable Market | Of those, how many will actually be paying us in five years? | The **plan** |

**The one rule we size by:**

> **Annual revenue pool = (number of qualified paying organizations) × (annual price they pay us).**

**What we deliberately do NOT count** — and this is where most sizing decks inflate by 10× or more:

- ❌ **The money flowing between our customers and their partners.** Commissions, royalties, payouts, revenue share, GMV. Reven governs that flow; **Reven does not earn a percentage of it.** A company that governs SAR 10 billion of partner payments and charges a fixed subscription has a subscription-sized market, not a SAR 10 billion market. Sizing decks that blur this are the ones that don't survive diligence.
- ❌ **"Influenced revenue."** If a partner influenced a deal, that deal's value is not our market.
- ❌ **Free invited counterparties.** A customer can invite its partners into Reven for free. Those partners improve the product's usefulness and contribute **zero revenue** until they independently buy.
- ❌ **Double-counting a company group.** One paying organization is counted once, even if it has twelve subsidiaries.
- ❌ **Multiplying Saudi by six to get "the GCC."** Every region below is counted separately and mutually exclusively.

This discipline makes our headline numbers smaller. That is intentional. **A number that survives scrutiny is worth more than a number that impresses briefly.**

## 1. The business model, in one page

Reven has **two commercial stages**, and confusing them is the single easiest way to misread this document.

### Stage 1 — Land: what we sell today

> **SAR 50 per seat, per month.** (≈ USD 13 per seat per month; **SAR 600 / USD 160 per seat per year.**)

That is deliberately cheap. It is a **land-and-expand** price, not a value-based price. Its job is to remove every reason to say no: no procurement committee, no business case, no six-month evaluation. A team of eight starts using it for **SAR 400 a month** — less than the cost of one lunch meeting about whether to buy it.

We are not trying to make money at this price. We are trying to **get inside the company and become the place the partnership record lives.**

### Stage 2 — Expand: what we sell once the intelligence layers ship

Once Reven carries the record of a company's partnerships, we can add the layers that are actually valuable — **partnership intelligence**: portfolio capacity, cross-company collaboration, outcome verification, benchmarking, and API access. Those are enterprise capabilities bought by executives with real budget, and they are priced as enterprise contracts.

> **⚠️ Pricing for the expansion tiers is not yet set.** This document models a **band** for them, drawn from the source report's module-and-attach-rate analysis and from comparable enterprise SaaS pricing. **Every expansion price in this document is an assumption marked [A], not an offer and not a validated price.** Establishing it is one of the named validation gates in §9.

### Why this two-stage structure is the whole investment case

Here is the number that matters most in this document:

| | Annual value of one customer |
|---|---|
| **Stage 1 — seats only** (18 seats, base case) | **SAR 10,800** (~$2,880) |
| **Stage 2 — full internal enterprise stack** (base case) [A] | **SAR 328,000** (~$87,000) |
| **The expansion multiple** | **≈ 30×** |

**In plain terms: we enter at a price nobody argues about, and the same customer can eventually be worth roughly thirty times that — without us ever taking a cut of their partner money.**

That is the bet. Everything below sizes it.

## 2. What counts as a customer

We only count an organization if it genuinely has the problem. The qualification test (from the source report) is that **most** of these are true:

1. It runs roughly **20–25 active partnerships or priority programs** — enough that memory and spreadsheets stop working.
2. **Three or more internal functions** are involved (commercial, finance, legal, operations) — so coordination is a real cost.
3. It has **external dependencies** — outcomes rely on parties outside the company.
4. There is a **recurring review** where partnership performance is discussed.
5. **Failure is expensive** — a missed commitment or a disputed payment has material consequence.
6. The current record is **fragmented** — no single trusted source.

Companies failing this test are excluded from every number in this document, however large they are. This is why our account counts are in the thousands, not the millions.

**How we counted Saudi Arabia** [F/A — fact-anchored, assumption-completed]:
Official-source proxy floor of ~232 large institutional entities; plus 5% of the 18,723 medium establishments recorded by GASTAT (~936); combined with other transparent institutional and enterprise proxies to ~1,168 — **rounded to a base of 1,200.** These lists overlap and are not deduplicated, which is why we model a wide range rather than a point estimate: **450 (low) / 1,200 (base) / 2,500 (high).**

## 3. What one customer pays us — the seat model

**Price:** SAR 600 per seat per year.
**The only variable:** how many people inside the customer use it.

| Case | Who's using it | Seats [A] | Annual value |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Low** | One partnership team only | **6** | **SAR 3,600** (~$960) |
| **Base** | Partnerships + finance + one more function | **18** | **SAR 10,800** (~$2,880) |
| **High** | Multi-function, multi-entity rollout | **45** | **SAR 27,000** (~$7,200) |

The seat range comes straight from the qualification test: an organization with 20–25 active partnerships and 3+ involved functions plausibly has 6 to 45 people who touch the record. **Seat counts are assumptions [A] until we have paying cohorts.**

> **The honest limitation of seat pricing, stated plainly.** At SAR 600 per seat per year, reaching an enterprise-sized contract on seats alone would require about **547 seats** in a single company. That will essentially never happen. **Seats cannot get us to enterprise revenue — only the intelligence layers can.** This is not a flaw in the plan; it *is* the plan. But it means the expansion layers are not upside to the story. **They are the story.** An investor should underwrite the layers, and treat the seat business as customer acquisition that happens to pay for part of itself.

## 4. Saudi Arabia — the sizing, step by step

### 4.1 Today's product (seats only)

| | Low | Base | High |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Qualified organizations | 450 | 1,200 | 2,500 |
| × Annual value per organization | SAR 3,600 | SAR 10,800 | SAR 27,000 |
| **= TAM (today's product)** | **SAR 1.62m** | **SAR 12.96m** | **SAR 67.5m** |
| *in USD* | *$0.43m* | *$3.46m* | *$18.0m* |

**Read this honestly: if every qualifying company in Saudi Arabia bought today's product at today's price, the entire market would be about SAR 13 million a year.** That is not a venture-scale market. We are stating it plainly because it is the correct number for what we sell *today*, and because the gap between it and the next table is the actual investment thesis.

### 4.2 The mature product (seats + partnership intelligence layers)

The source report models five monetizable layers. Layers 2–5 are **earned** — they require the product, the trust and the network to exist first. They are option value, not current revenue.

| Layer | What the customer buys | Base-case Saudi pool [A] |
|---|---|---:|
| **1. Single-company intelligence** | The core record and portfolio management | **SAR 393.6m** |
| **2. Cross-company coordination** | Working with counterparties inside Reven (55% attach) | **SAR 49.5m** |
| **3. Matchmaking / membership** | Finding and qualifying new partners (15% attach) | **SAR 13.5m** |
| **4. Verified outcome orchestration** | Fixed-price workspace per program — **no take rate, no success fee** | **SAR 60m** |
| **5. Graph / benchmark / API** | Permissioned benchmarks and data access | **SAR 30m** |
| **Mature Saudi total** | Counted once, no double-counting | **SAR 546.6m** (~$145.8m) |

Sensitivity: **SAR 71.5m (low) / SAR 546.6m (base) / SAR 2.948bn (high).**

### 4.3 The Saudi ceiling, in one line

| | Base case |
|---|---:|
| Today's product ceiling | **SAR 12.96m** |
| Mature product ceiling | **SAR 546.6m** |
| **Multiple** | **≈ 42×** |

**Why 42× and not the 30× from §1?** Two different things are being measured, and both are true. The **30×** is how much more *one customer* pays. The **42×** is how much bigger the *whole market* gets — larger, because the mature model adds outcome-workspace and API units that are not tied one-to-one to core accounts. **Per-customer value grows 30×; the market pool grows 42×.**

It applies to **every sector where one party earns money because of another party's contribution** — insurance commission, franchise royalty, travel commission, freight agency settlement, construction flow-through, marketplace take rate, dealer commission. The qualified Saudi account base spreads across the economy accordingly.

*(Mature pool here uses the core + coordination bundle at SAR 369,250 weighted annual value per account [A]; the outcome-workspace and API layers in §4.2 sit on top of this and are not allocated by sector.)*

**Three things a generalist investor should take from this table.**

1. **No single industry is more than 15% of the market.** That is diversification most early companies do not have — a construction downturn does not end the business.
2. **Technology is only the third-largest slice**, at 12.5%. The market is not a software-industry market; it is an economy-wide one.
3. **One compliance investment unlocks all of them.** Saudi Arabia's e-invoicing mandate, withholding tax rules and data-protection law apply to *every* industry identically. We do not have to rebuild the product per sector to sell across sectors — a point developed in `Reven_Economic_Trends_and_Why_Now.md`.

**Go-to-market note, so this is not misread:** a horizontal *product* does not mean a scattered *sales effort*. The plan is to sell into **two chosen industries first** and let the rest arrive inbound. The table shows where the market is, not where the sales team goes on Monday.

## 8. The value ceiling: what one customer can become

This is the "expansion and value capture" question, answered directly.

### 8.1 The expansion ladder

| Stage | What they're paying for | Annual value (base) [A] | Multiple vs. entry |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| **1. Land — one team** | 6 seats | SAR 3,600 | 1× |
| **2. Grow — department** | 18 seats | SAR 10,800 | 3× |
| **3. Grow — multi-function** | 45 seats | SAR 27,000 | 7.5× |
| **4. Expand — core intelligence** | + portfolio capacity module | ~SAR 127,500 | 35× |
| **5. Expand — full internal stack** | + intelligence, governance, integration | **SAR 328,000** | **91×** |
| **6. Expand — + cross-company** | + external collaboration | **SAR 369,250** | **103×** |
| **7. Ceiling — high case** | Large multi-entity enterprise | **SAR 862,000** | **239×** |

*Multiples are against the 6-seat entry point. Against the 18-seat base entry, stage 5 is **≈30×** — the figure quoted in §1.*

### 8.2 What share of the customer's money we take

We do **not** take a percentage of our customers' partner payments. Ever. So our value capture must be measured against the **cost we remove**, not the money we touch.

A qualified organization coordinating 20–25 partnerships across 3+ functions plausibly spends **2–5 full-time-equivalents** on partnership coordination. At a Saudi loaded cost of roughly SAR 250,000–400,000 per FTE, that is a **SAR 500,000 – 2,000,000 annual coordination cost base** [A].

| Stage | We charge | Customer's coordination cost base [A] | **We capture** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Seat stage | SAR 10,800 | ~SAR 1,000,000 | **~1%** |
| Enterprise stage | SAR 328,000 | ~SAR 3,000,000 *(larger org)* | **~11%** |

**Why this matters:** at 1% of the cost base, the entry price is beneath the threshold at which anyone builds a business case — that is why it lands. At ~11%, the enterprise price still leaves the overwhelming majority of the value with the customer — which is why it renews. **A vendor capturing 10–15% of a cost base it verifiably reduces is in a durable position. A vendor taking a percentage of its customers' revenue is in an adversarial one.**

### 8.3 Why we refuse take-rate pricing, in investor terms

We could charge a percentage of the partner payments we govern. It would produce a bigger headline number. We do not, for three reasons:

1. **It destroys neutrality.** Reven's product is a number *two companies both accept*. A referee taking a cut of the score is not a referee.
2. **It is the pricing model finance buyers most resent** — and finance is our expansion buyer.
3. **It changes how we are valued.** Fixed recurring software is valued as software. Transaction-linked revenue is valued as payments, at materially lower multiples.

## 9. What has to be true (the validation gates)

Every material number here is an assumption until tested. These are the tests, in order.

| # | Assumption | How we test it | If it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **A named 1,200-account Saudi list actually exists** | Build the list; deduplicate groups and subsidiaries | The base case drops toward the 450 low case |
| 2 | **Seats per account average ~18** | Measure across the first 30 paying accounts | Seat-stage revenue rescales linearly |
| 3 | **SAR 50/seat converts without a sales motion** | Self-serve signup funnel; measure activation without human touch | If it needs a rep, the unit economics break and the price must rise |
| 4 | **15–20% of landed accounts convert to enterprise** | Cohort tracking from first landing | **This is the single most important number in the model** — SOM moves almost proportionally |
| 5 | **Enterprise tiers can be priced near the modelled band** | 30 priced interviews, 10 written offers, then paid pilots | The 30× expansion multiple compresses, and with it the whole thesis |
| 6 | **Customers stay** (retention) | Cohort renewal at 12 and 24 months | Everything downstream is invalid |
| 7 | **The layers are buildable on pre-seed capital** | Release gates against the SAR 2m plan | Sequence must lengthen; do not fund the network before wedge proof |

> **Gate 4 and Gate 5 are the thesis.** Everything else adjusts a number. Those two decide whether there is a business. An investor should ask about them first, and should expect us to have cohort data before claiming either.

## 10. Sensitivity and the anti-inflation rules we hold ourselves to

**Sensitivity is linear and compounding:** a 25% reduction in qualified accounts *or* in price reduces the pool by 25%. A 25% reduction in **both** reduces it by **43.75%.** Our low case already applies pessimistic assumptions to both simultaneously, which is why the low-to-high spread is wide.

**The rules we do not break:**

- **Conversion and activation affect SOM, not TAM.** If customers convert poorly, that shrinks what we obtain — it does not shrink the size of the problem, and we will not pretend otherwise in either direction.
- **If external activation is weak, we remove layer 2** rather than inflating subscription counts to compensate.
- **No take rate, success fee, or contingent outcome fee is modelled or recommended.** Outcome verification is sold as fixed subscription or workspace pricing.
- **Free invited partners contribute zero revenue.** They make the product better. They are not customers.
- **The high case is a stress test, not a forecast.** We do not lead with it.

## 11. What we are explicitly not claiming

Stated plainly, because these are the claims that usually get made and later unwound:

- We are **not** claiming the partner payment flows we govern (billions of riyals) as our market.
- We are **not** claiming today's product is a venture-scale market. **At SAR 50 per seat, it is not** — Saudi TAM for today's product is about SAR 13 million a year, and we say so.
- We are **not** claiming validated enterprise pricing. It is unset, and modelled as a band.
- We are **not** claiming network effects. There is no network yet, so no network-driven acquisition cost is assumed anywhere in this model.
- We are **not** claiming the $49.5bn global high case is achievable. It is the arithmetic ceiling of the method, included for completeness and transparency.
- We are **not** using public SaaS revenue multiples to value a product still in build.

## 12. The whole argument in six sentences

Reven charges **SAR 50 per seat per month** — a price low enough that a qualified company can start without a business case, a committee, or a procurement cycle. At that price the entire Saudi market for today's product is roughly **SAR 13 million a year**, which we state openly because it is true and because it is the point: the entry price is customer acquisition, not the business. Once Reven holds the record of how a company's partnerships actually perform, we add the partnership intelligence layers — portfolio capacity, cross-company coordination, outcome verification, benchmarking — which are enterprise capabilities bought by executives, and which take the same customer from **SAR 10,800 to roughly SAR 328,000 a year, about 30×**. That expansion moves the Saudi market ceiling from SAR 13 million to roughly **SAR 547 million**, the global ceiling to roughly **$7.3 billion**, and produces a base-case five-year business of about **$22 million of recurring revenue from around 1,000 landed customers, of which 169 have converted**. The market spreads across eleven industries with no single one above 15%, because the problem — one party earning money from another party's contribution, and both needing to agree the number — is economy-wide rather than technology-specific, and because Saudi Arabia's e-invoicing, withholding tax and data-protection rules apply to every one of those industries identically, so a single compliance investment opens all of them. **The two things that decide whether this works are the share of landed accounts that convert to enterprise contracts, and the price those contracts actually bear — and we will have cohort evidence on both before we ask anyone to underwrite them.**
